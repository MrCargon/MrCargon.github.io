Artificial Life

Two cellular automata, one lineage. Conway's Game of Life is the discrete original: cells are alive or dead. Lenia makes everything continuous and organisms appear. No sprites, no textures, no audio files — every pixel and every sound here is generated from maths at runtime.

Custom colour

Three stops for the value ramp: dead cells, the midtone the value passes through, and the brightest live cells. Editing any of them switches the preset list to “Custom”.

And a gradient inside every single cell: inner is the centre, outer is the rim, Edge is where one becomes the other, and Glow bleeds colour into the empty space around living cells. Turn Cell shading up and zoom in — at 1× a cell is roughly one screen pixel, so this is detail that appears as you magnify.

Conway — the discrete original

Each cell looks at its eight neighbours. A live cell with 2 or 3 live neighbours survives; a dead cell with exactly 3 is born; everything else dies. Written B3/S23. Every cell must read the previous generation — updating in place gives a different automaton entirely, and double buffering is part of the rule rather than an optimisation.

That is enough to be Turing complete. A glider is a packet travelling at a known speed, a glider gun is a clock, and two gliders meeting at the right phase annihilate — which gives NOT, AND and OR. Distance is the wiring: a channel one cell too long shifts the phase and the gate stops working. Nothing was added to the rules to allow this; it took decades to find.

B3/S23 is one point in a space of 262,144 rules. Switch universes above: HighLife (B36/S23) contains a genuine self-replicator, Seeds (B2/S) lets nothing survive at all and detonates, Day & Night behaves identically on a live background as on a dead one.

Lenia — what happens when you make it continuous

Lenia makes everything smooth: cell values, the neighbourhood, and time itself. That smoothness is what lets organisms appear — shapes with a boundary that hold themselves together, move, and reproduce.

Each step convolves the grid with a smooth ring kernel, then applies a bell-curve growth function. A ring, not a disc: a creature's centre sees a complete ring and holds steady while its edge sees a partial one and grows or dies. That single detail is the difference between organisms and mush.

The habitable band is narrow. Slide μ too far and the world goes sterile within a few hundred steps, or floods and saturates. Radius 10 lives; radius 9, with everything else identical, goes extinct.

The same family

Conway is the discrete corner of the space Lenia occupies continuously. Between them sit Larger than Life rules, where the neighbourhood grows past a single ring of cells but the states stay binary. Switching modes above moves along that axis — the machinery underneath is the same: a neighbourhood sum, a rule applied to it, and a double buffer.

Life inside Life

The OTCA metapixel (Brice Due, 2006) is a Conway pattern that behaves like a single Conway cell — 2048×2048 cells wide, one of its generations taking 35,328 of ours. Tile the plane with them and you get Life whose cells are themselves Life, all the way down.

Not simulated here, deliberately: a 10×10 metapixel board is about 4×1011 cell updates per meta-generation. That needs Hashlife and a desktop, not a canvas on a portfolio page — and saying so is more useful than shipping something that pretends.

Lenia was devised by Bert Chan. Both simulations run as GPU fragment shaders over ping-pong render targets — Lenia takes roughly 300 neighbourhood samples per cell per step, which is hopeless in JavaScript and trivial for a graphics card. Every Conway pattern here is verified by simulation in tests/verify-patterns.cjs rather than trusted: the R-pentomino settles at generation 1103, the acorn at 5206, diehard vanishes at 130 — all measured.
