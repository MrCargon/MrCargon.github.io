Artificial Life

How it works

Conway's Game of Life gives each cell two states, alive or dead. Lenia makes everything continuous — cell values, the neighbourhood, time itself. That smoothness is what lets organisms appear: shapes with a boundary that hold themselves together, move, and reproduce.

Each step convolves the grid with a smooth ring kernel, then applies a bell-curve growth function. A ring, not a disc: a creature's centre sees a complete ring and holds steady while its edge sees a partial one and grows or dies. That single detail is the difference between organisms and mush.

The habitable band is narrow. Slide μ too far and the world goes sterile within a few hundred steps, or floods and saturates. Radius 10 lives; radius 9, with everything else identical, goes extinct.

Lenia was devised by Bert Chan. Implemented here as a GPU fragment shader over ping-pong render targets — roughly 300 neighbourhood samples per cell per step, which is hopeless in JavaScript and trivial for a graphics card.
